Max Planck's 1901 paper *Ueber das Gesetz der Energieverteilung im Normalspectrum* (*On the Law of Energy Distribution in the Normal Spectrum*) is generally considered the foundation of modern Quantum Mechanics. The benchmark separating the classical, continuous physics of mechanics, electrodynamics, and thermodynamics developed in the 17th-19th centuries from modern physics based on quanta of mass, energy, charge, and radiation. The story, as it is often recounted, goes that at the time the Rayleigh-Jeans law was an accurate description of Blackbody-Radiation at large wavelengths, but began to diverge at shorter ones. This lead to a theoretical conundrum known as the *Ultraviolet Catastrophe*, which Planck solved in a stroke of genius by quantizing the the allowed energy-states of a large group of individual thermal resonators. A closer historical examination, however, reveals a much messier picture. 

While the Rayleigh-Jeans law was derived a year before his paper in 1900, Planck was actually trying to derive an earlier, better blackbody radiation relation: Wien's law. This relation had the opposite problem to the Rayleigh-Jeans law; it fit short-wavelength data but began to diverge at longer ones. Moreover, Planck's original motivation was to disprove the discretized-theory of matter (atoms) presented by Ludwig Boltzmann at the time in favor of a continuous picture. He actually succeeded in deriving Wien's law this way, and then corrected his derivation for long-wavelength data to arrive at the correct "Planck's Law" without introducing quanta. However, his work was based on flawed thermodynamic arguments. His 1901 paper was a second attempt to derive this equation from first principles. Even here, Planck did not go so far as to claim the energy itself was quantized, but introduced the procedure as a mathematical method to calculate the system's entropy. He would only go so far as to claim that the *interaction* between the resonators and ambient radiation could be discretized. It was Albert Einstein who four years later would take the idea of quantization as an intrinsic property of nature. 

In my opinion, this view of Planck's derivation paints a more-honest picture of theoretical discoveries. It was firmly rooted in the recent experimental advances to measure blackbody radiation. His original motivations went against our modern picture of physics, and at first he managed to get the right result, albeit for the wrong reasons. As newer, long-wavelength data began to contradict Wien's Law and other arguments chipped away at his derivation, he was intellectually honest enough to try a statistical-mechanics approach. Even after he re-derived the correct equation by quantization, it would be several years before others grasped the deeper physical meaning of his work. To better understand the twists and turns of Quantum Mechanics' birth, we must begin with the experimental advances that let researchers measure a blackbody-radiation curve. 

## The Experimental Underpinnings of Planck's Derivation
#### The Quest for A Perfect Blackbody 
In 1859 the german physicist Gustav Kirchhoff used thermodynamic thought-experiments to prove that for a material in thermal equilibrium, the ratio of its absorption $a(\nu, T)$ and emission $e(\nu, T)$ is a universal ratio $u(\nu, T)$ that is *independent* of the material in question, depending only on the frequency of light and temperature of the material. It was akin to someone proving that an ideal gas's volume $V(P)$ and temperature $T(P)$ would always maintain a constant ratio without specifying the ideal gas law ($PV = nRT$). Importantly, for a *blackbody* material that absorbs all light ($a=1$), this ratio is its emission spectrum $e(\nu, T)=u(\nu, T)$. Understanding this universal nature of blackbody-radiation become a promising frontier of physics (boy were they right!), leading experimentalists to expend significant effort to realize the ideal blackbody-radiator. 

The first attempts revolved around coating metallic sheets in soot or metallic oxides because black materials absorb more light (thus *blackbody*). Unfortunately, these materials that appeared black in the visible spectrum had very different absorption properties in the infrared, and their absorption characteristics would change with temperature. The limited wavelengths and temperatures available through these coating methods ultimately led to their abandonment. 

The breakthrough came nearly thirty years later in 1895 from two researchers at the *Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt* (PTR) in Berlin, Wilhelm Wien and Otto Lummer. PTR played the lead role in producing the blackbody-radiation data upon which Planck would ultimately base his work. Instead of searching for a material with their desired absorption characteristics, they focused instead on developing a system capable of maintaining thermodynamic equilibrium between its temperature and emitted radiation: a hollow cavity. Their experimental innovation was adding of a hole small enough so that the escaped radiation was too negligible to affect this equilibrium, while admitting a small amount of light into the cavity that was effectively trapped inside. Even if a material had an absorption $a<1$, after successive reflections and partial absorptions from the cavity's internal walls the light would be completely absorbed. This way, the hollow cavity + aperture behaved like a perfect blackbody radiator with $a=1$, and the emitted light was a pristine sample of the universal ratio $u(\nu, T)$ Kirchhoff predicted. 

Lummer and another researcher Ernst Pringsheim initially built spherical and cylindrical cavities out of copper, iron, or porcelain. They immersed their cavities in various fluid baths to achieve different temperatures, including liquid air, boiling water, and molten saltpeter, achieving temperature ranges from $-188^\circ$C to $700^\circ$C. A little later, in 1898, PTR researchers extended their temperature range up to $1500^\circ$C using an electrically heated platinum cavity. With the blackbody radiator developed, the PTR researchers were halfway there; now they had to actually measure the radiation. 
#### Measuring Radiant Power  
The blackbody cavities emitted very small amounts of radiation, forcing the PTR researchers Lummer and Ferdinand Kurlbaum to develop a very precise "spectrobolometer" to measure small changes in the emission's intensities at specific wavelengths. The bolometer, first invented by American astronomer Samuel Pierpont Langley in 1878 to precisely measure temperatures, consisted of several strips of metal coordinated like a Wheatstone bridge circuit. It consists of four resistors, two with known resistances $R_1, R_2$ and one with a variable resistance $R_3'$, to measure the fourth one's unknown resistance. $R_3'$ is tuned until the voltage $V_g$ between the two midpoints is $0$, leading to the relationship $R_x = R_3' R_2/ R_1$.  In PTR's case, the four 'resistors' were four narrow strips of platinum. The fourth "unknown resistor" strip was exposed to the radiation, and its resistance changed proportionally to how much it temperature. Because $R_1 = R_2$, the third strip's temperature was adjusted by a current $I$ causing Joule heating ($I^2 R$) until the bridge was balanced. The resistance and current supplied gave an exact conversion to the total absorbed radiant power. 

Their spectrobolometer had two main weaknesses. The first was thermal inertia, the time it took for the fourth strip to reach thermal equilibrium with the radiation. Langley's original design took around 100 seconds to get an accurate measurement with $10^{-5}$C precision. To address this, Lummer an Kurlbaum created incredibly thin platinum strips using the following method. They welded a platinum sheet to a silver sheet ten times thicker, and then rolled this sandwich until the platinum got down to at $10^{-6}$m thickness. The strips were cut from the sheet, and then the silver was chemically etched away using nitric acid. This reduced the equilibrium time to eight seconds for similarly precise measurements. 

The spectrobolometer's second weakness was the absorption of the platinum strip. It lacked high absorption in the infrared, leading to systematic underestimation of the actual radiation power. Thy addressed this by blackening the strip using *Platinmoor*, a pourous electrolytically deposited form of platinum. Nonetheless, Kurlbaum would still apply a systematic 2.5% correction to their data to account for the under-absorbed radiation. 

## Early Data and The First Derivations
#### The first near-IR measurements and Wien's Law
With their perfect blackbody and spectrobolometer ready to roll, PTR researchers began sampling the universal ratio $u(\nu, T)$ up to $6\mu$m-wavelength light and temperature ranges from  $-188^\circ$C to $700^\circ$C. Commonly used optical glass was highly absorptive at longer wavelengths, so instead they used silver mirrors to collect and focus the blackbody's radiation. They also specially fabricated dielectric prisms for infrared wavelength selection. 

In 1896 Wilhelm Wien, who helped design the blackbody radiator, derived a distribution that perfectly fit the data collected *up until that time*. His full derivation is a little beyond the scope of this history, but its broad strokes are as follows. He considered a blackbody cavity at temperature $T$ with one wall as moveable piston.  Under adiabatic compression of this cavity, its temperature would shift, and to ensure no heat is exchanged the blackbody radiation distribution $u(\nu, T)$ would have to shift as well. Wien proved that the term $\nu^3 V$ was an invariant quantity under such compression. Moreover, radiation was known at the time to exert pressure $p=u/3$, proportional to $u=\int u(\nu, T) d\nu$ the total power-per-unit-area. Wien compared the work done by this pressure during an adiabatic expansion and the changes in the spectral density from his invariant term to arrive at a differential relationship 
$$ \delta u_\nu = \left( \frac{\nu}{3} \frac{\delta u_\nu}{\delta \nu} - u_\nu\right)\frac{\delta V}{V}. $$
The general solution to this equation takes the form $u_\nu = \nu^3 \phi(\nu^3 V)$, where $\phi$ is an undetermined function. In an adiabatic expansion, temperature and volume are related as $V \propto T^{-3}$, which yielded the general form $$u(\nu, T) = \nu^3 f(\nu/T). $$This is known today as *Wein's Displacement law*. Using the relationship $u_\nu \nu = - u_\lambda \lambda$ and $\nu = c/\lambda$ the relation can be re-written in terms of wavelengths as $u(\lambda, T) = \lambda^{-5} \psi(\lambda T)$. Taking the derivative of this general form and finding its roots show that the maximum-intensity wavelength of the spectrum scales inversely with temperature $\lambda_{max} T = \text{const.}$. 

Everything up until here has withstood the test of time, but while Wien's next step of deducing the exact form of $f(\nu/T)$ fit their data, it was built on shaky theoretical foundations. Wien proposed a *Molecular Hypothesis*, that the blackbody's radiation was emitted by a gas of molecules in thermal equilibrium. Up until now, Wien had followed the tenet of Kirchhoff's theorem that the universal ratio is independent of material or composition by using general thermodynamic and electrodynamic arguments. He now turned to a specific microscopic model, and if that wasn't bad enough, he committed an even graver mistake by guessing that a molecule's emitted wavelength $\lambda$ is a function of the its velocity $v$. Wien readily admitted there was no rigorous electrodynamic mechanism and this was a heuristic guess, relying primarily on an "ends justify the means" argument to justify it.

Wien made this leap in order to provide a direct mapping between  the emission spectrum  andthe Boltzmann velocity distribution for a molecular gas, where the percentage of molecules with velocities between $v$ and $v + dv$ is $v^2 \exp(-\alpha v^2/T)$ ($\alpha$ is a constant). He postulated the wavelength distribution has a similar form $u(\lambda, T)= F(\lambda) \exp[-f(\lambda)/T]$, with two unknown functions $F(\lambda), f(\lambda)$. By comparing this to the previously found general form, and imposing that the distribution's integration must follow the Stephan Boltzmann law $u \propto T^4$, he concluded the distribution must have a form $u(\lambda, T) = c_1/\lambda^5 \exp( -c_2 /\lambda T)$, with two unknown constants $c_1$ and $c_2$. Expressed again in frequency form, he arrived at the Wien approximation: $$ u(\nu, T) = \alpha \nu^3 \exp\left( - \frac{\beta \nu}{T} \right) $$Despite its shaky theoretical foundation, this derivation fit the experimental data up to $6\mu m$ exceptionally well,  Here enters our main protagonist: Max Planck. Impressed by the experimental agreement but bothered by Wien's molecular hypothesis, he soon began working on a more generalized thermodynamic derivations for blackbody radiation. 

#### Planck's Commitment to the 2nd Law and Opposition to Boltzmann
Planck was deeply ideologically committed to a continuous, thermodynamic view of the the microscopic world. In particular, he believed Wein's law could be derived from the second law of thermodynamics, which states that entropy of an isolated system can either stay the same or increase, but never decrease. In the past few decades, this viewpoint had made him very critical of Ludwig Boltzmann's atomic theory and statistical interpretation of the second law. 

> When correctly used, the second principle of the mechanical theory of heat is incompatible with the hypothesis of finite atoms. One should therefore expect that in the course of the further development of the theory, there will be a fight between these two hypotheses that will cost the life of one of them. It would be premature to predict the outcome of this fight now; but for the moment it seems to me that, in spite of the great successes of the atomic theory in the past, we will finally have to give it up and to decide in favor of the assumption of continuous matter. (Planck, 1882)

In Boltzmann's kinetic theory of gasses, spontaneous decreases of entropy are not forbidden, just exceedingly unlikely. For example, the chance that all the gas molecules in a room spontaneously reduce their entropy by congregating in one corner is exceedingly low (so low that it most likely hasn't happened since the start of the universe), but not strictly speaking impossible. This kind of theoretical exception to the 2nd law, including thought experiments like *Maxwell's Demon*, motivated Planck to attempt a more generalized thermodynamic derivation of Wien's law. 

Planck's first important choice was how to model the blackbody's interaction between the thermal energy and radiation intensity. Because Kirchhoff's theorem was independent of material choice or its properties, Planck assumed that any physically valid model (which Wien's molecular hypothesis was not) capable of describing this interaction would yield the correct result. He turned to Heinrich Hertz's "resonator" experiments from a decade earlier, the first apparatus that controlled electric dipoles to generate and measure electromagnetic waves. 

Planck modeled the cavity's wall as many "Hertzian resonators", linear harmonic oscillators with oppositely charged poles. Today, this choice is framed as a simplification of an atomic model, but as previously mentioned Planck's motivation was precisely the opposite. Planck used the model for its physical validity and mathematical simplicity, making no assumptions about the internal structure or size of these oscillators, and it would be over a decade until Millikan's oil drop experiment proved the quantization of charge in 1913. Moreover, Planck completely skipped past the statistical-mechanical equipartition theorem (which came from Boltzmann's kinetic theory) which gave a clear relationship between the resonator's energy and temperature: $U = k_B T$. Instead, Planck sought to derive the entropy and energy of a Hertzian resonator to define the temperature in strict thermodynamic terms: $\frac{dS}{dU} =\frac{1}{T}$. 

#### Planck's First Derivation: Entropy Must Increase! 
In 1899 Planck modeled his oscillators as a time-dependent dipole moment $f(t)$ with resonant frequency $\nu$, driven by an external electric field $Z(t)$: $$\frac{d^2f}{dt^2} + 2\sigma \nu \frac{df}{dt} + 4\pi^2 \nu^2 f = \frac{3c^3\sigma}{4\pi\nu}Z ,$$where $\sigma$ is the logarithmic damping rate governing the loss of energy via the emission of outgoing spherical electromagnetic waves. Planck purposefully omitted the mass and charge of the resonator by grouping those effects under $\sigma$ to focus on the dipole's electrodynamic properties. The total energy of the oscillator is the sum of its kinetic and potential energies: $$U = \frac{1}{2} Kf^2+\frac{1}{2}L \left( \frac{df}{dt} \right)^2, $$where $K=16\pi^4\nu^3/3c^3\sigma$ and $L=K/4\pi^2\nu^2$. 

Hertz had shown in his experiments that the intensity distribution of the emitted radiation had an angular component to it, $I = \frac{\nu^2}{c^2} U\sin\theta$. Planck used this to define equilibrium between the resonator and an ambient radiation field. By demanding that the total energy flux across a spherical boundary emitted from the oscillating dipole was equivalent to the amount of energy absorbed by the oscillator, he derived a relationship between the spectral energy density of the radiation $u_\nu$ and the average energy of the oscillator: $$u_\nu = \frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}U. $$Planck was emboldened by the fact that this relationship was independent of $\sigma$, decoupling the light-matter interaction from the macroscopic properties of the matter in question. He now invoked Wien's displacement law to rewrite $U = \nu f(\nu/T)$. Using the first law of thermodynamics to equate $dU = TdS$, he showed that the entropy must be a function of $\nu/T$, or by using the previous equation, dependent on an unknown function $f^{-1} (U/\nu)$ of the ratio $U/\nu$. 

Planck then enforced the second law onto his system. His overall strategy was as follows: a resonator began at the equilibrium energy $U_0$ and was then perturbed by a small $\Delta U \ll U_0$. Over a small period of time $dt$ the resonator should relax by some amount infinitesimal amount $\delta U \ll \Delta U$ by emitting radiation, and in doing so the overall entropy of the system should increase. 

A small change $\Delta U$ leads to an equivalent change in the emitted radiation $\Delta I = \frac{\nu^2}{c^2}\Delta U \sin\theta$. During the relaxation time, the energy lost by the resonator $\delta U$ must balance the change in intensity integrated over the full solid angle $\Omega$: $$\delta U = -dt \int d\Omega \Delta I = - \frac{8\pi}{3}\frac{\nu^2}{c^2} \Delta U dt.$$Now with an expression for the infinitesimal energy relaxation, Planck sought to relate this back to the overall entropy change. He defined an *entropy-intensity* function $L(I)$ to describe the entropy of the surrounding radiation and applied a Taylor approximation: $$ \Delta L=\frac{dL}{dI}\bigg|_{I_0}\Delta I + \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2L}{dI^2}\bigg|_{I_0}(\Delta I)^2. $$He similarly Taylor expanded the resonator's entropy: $$dS = \frac{dS}{dU}\bigg|_{U_0 + \Delta U}\delta U = \frac{dS}{dU}\bigg|_{U_0}\delta U + \frac{d^2S}{dU^2}\bigg|_{U_0}\delta U\Delta U.$$The overall entropy change can be fully written out as: $dS_{tot} = dS + dt \int d\Omega \Delta L \geq 0$. Integrating the linear term in $\Delta L$'s approximation is equivalent to the expression earlier for $\delta U$ with an additional factor of $\frac{dL}{dI}|_{I_0}$,  and using a similar substitution the quadratic term becomes $-\frac{2}{5} \frac{\nu^2}{c^2} \frac{d^2L}{dI^2}|_{I_0} \Delta U \delta U$. Thus, the overall entropy change can be rewritten as $$dS_{tot} = \left( \frac{dS}{dU}\bigg|_{U_0} - \frac{dL}{dI}\bigg|_{I_0} \right)\delta U+ \left( \frac{d^2S}{dU^2}\bigg|_{U_0} - \frac{2}{5} \frac{\nu^2}{c^2}\frac{d^2L}{dI^2}\bigg|_{I_0} \right)\Delta U \delta U. $$For the system to evolve towards equilibrium, the initial energy kick $\Delta U$ and the subsequent relaxation $\delta U$ must have opposite signs, but which one is negative or positive is not fixed. For $dS_{tot}$ to be positive definite, this means that the first term must cancel to zero. For this to hold for all possible values of $U,I$ related by the dipole radiation implies a general form for $L(I)$: $$\frac{dS}{dU}\bigg|_{U_0} =\frac{dL}{dI}\bigg|_{I_0} \Longrightarrow L(I) = \frac{\nu^2}{c^2}S\left(\frac{c^2}{\nu^2} I \right) + \text{const.} $$Which means we can re-express the second derivative of $L(I)$ as $\frac{d^2L}{dI^2}\bigg|_{I_0}=\frac{c^2}{\nu^2}\frac{d^2S}{dU^2}\bigg|_{U_0}$. Using this relation to rewrite the total entropy change, we arrive at an important Benchmark of Planck's original derivation: $$dS_{tot} = \frac{3}{5}\frac{d^2S}{dU^2}\Delta U \delta U \geq 0. $$Because the product of the initial energy perturbation $\Delta U$ and relaxation $\delta U$ will always be negative, the second derivative must represent a strictly negative function of entropy: $\frac{d^2S}{dU^2}=-f(U)$ to follow the second law. 

To deduce the exact form of $f(U)$, Planck introduced a somewhat flawed *additivity argument*. He considered $n$ oscillators each with energy $U$ undergoing this relaxation process, and assumed that their total entropy change is equivalent to $n$ times a single resonator's entropy change. Erroneously, he went on to equate this with the entropy change of a single resonator of energy $nU$ undergoing the perturbation $n\Delta U$. Under this flawed reasoning, the entropy function should satisfy the relation $f(nU) = f(U)/n$. The only solution to such a relation is $f(U) = \alpha/U$, where $\alpha$ is a constant. He arrived at the expression $$\frac{d^2S}{dU^2} = -\frac{\alpha}{U} \Longrightarrow S(U) = -\frac{U}{a \nu}\ln\left(\frac{U}{b\nu}\right),$$where the $\nu$'s were included fro his previous reasoning that the entropy must be a function of $U/\nu$ from Wien's displacement law. Differentiating this relation to get the temperature $\frac{1}{T} = -\frac{1}{a\nu} \left( \ln \frac{U}{b\nu} + 1\right)$, doing some rearranging, and introducing the fundamental relation found earlier $u_\nu = \frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}U$ ultimately yielded Wien's spectral distribution: $$u(\nu, T) = \frac{8 \pi b \nu^3}{c^3} \exp\left( - \frac{a\nu}{T} \right). $$Having largely avoided microscopic descriptions of a blackbody, Planck had presented a derivation of Wien's law as a necessary consequence of the Second Law of Thermodynamics. However, soon criticisms of his derivation would begin piling up. 

## Getting the Right Answer, For The Wrong Reasons

Two main criticisms quickly emerged of Planck's derivation that forced him to rework, but not abandon, parts of the derivation. While people largely held Wien's law to be true, Planck could often lean on an "ends justify the means" to smooth over the wrinkles other found in his work. 
##### The Time-Reversibility of Electrodynamics 
The earliest criticism came from Planck's ideological nemesis: Ludwig Boltzmann. Before using a statistical-mechanic approach to develop his kinetic theory of gasses, Boltzmann had tried  to derive the Boltzmann velocity distribution using an analogous approach through the second law of thermodynamics. He developed an H-function based on the molecular velocities, showed how through molecular collisions its first derivative $dH/dt\leq 0$, and also that it corresponded to $H \propto -S$. At its minimum (entropic maximum), the velocity distribution matched his expectations. Shortly after publishing his work, physicist Josef Loschmidt pointed out that the laws of mechanics were just as valid in reverse, $t \to -t$, and following Boltzmann's argument reversing all the molecular velocities should lead to an overall *decrease* in entropy, showing how the H-function could disobey the second law. At first, Boltzmann introduced a *molecular chaos* hypothesis that the velocities of two colliding particles were uncorrelated to break this time reversal symmetry. Uncomfortable with this ad-hoc constraint, Boltzmann eventually turned to combinatoric arguments to develop a statistical-mechanical derivation of his velocity distribution. 

Planck modeled the equilibrium of his resonators as the interplay between absorbing plane-wave radiation and emitting spherical dipole-radiation waves. Boltzmann immediately recognized a similar flaw as his H-function. As the laws of electrodynamics are also time-reversible, it was also possible for the resonator to absorb a spherical wave and emit a plane-wave. Following Planck's derivation, such a process should *decrease* the blackbody's entropy, violating the second law. Planck originally responded by claiming that such spherical waves were *nonphysical* because they required coordinated boundary conditions at infinity. Boltzmann responded that this was no more than an ad-hoc selection of initial conditions, meaning that the irreversibility was not a necessary consequence of the second law, but only highly unlikely. 

Planck addressed this argument by adding a *natürliche Strahlung* (natural radiation) initial hypothesis. He postulated that the independent Fourier components making up the exciting electric field posessed rapidly varying, uncorrelated phases. These fluctuations destroyed the phase-coherence required for converging spherical waves, breaking this time-reversal symmetry and ensuring a deterministic evolution of the system. Boltzmann's criticism led Planck to modify his derivation to include some qualifications, but did not convince him to abandon the approach altogether. 

##### Additivity Argument 
Wien and other researchers at PTR were quick to point out a
